embeddings · the explainer

What a player vector is.

This map measures how players play, not how good they are.

An embedding is a player-season turned into 64 numbers — a position in a space where nearby means "played a similar style." The rest of this page is about keeping two different assets straight, and about the exact boundary of what the model can see.

Plate 01 · the two assets — do not conflate them

There are two different "vectors" in this project, and past drafts of this site mixed them up. Here they are, separated:

assetwhat it is
assets/mtnn_embeddings.f32The production 64-d embeddings — the MTNN v5 model's own output, 12,966 player-seasons × 64 numbers. This is what the games, the twins, and the trails actually query. Single-model serving.
assets/vectors.jsonNOT a projection of the embedding. It holds the 14 raw per-100 game stats per player-season, z-scored within season — the model's input view, not its output. Useful for inspection; never the thing being served.

The atlas's x/y/z coordinates are a PCA(3) of those 14 stats — a 3-number view that keeps 62.4% of the stats' variance, drawn so humans can look at the cloud. It is a visualization aid, not the model. The model lives in 64-d; the atlas is a shadow it casts for your eyes.

sources: asset roles and PCA figures per overhaul plan §1.3 and project records (14 stats z-scored within season; Atlas xyz = PCA(3), 62.4% variance kept, labeled a view). Full ledger: /provenance.

Plate 02 · stat scope, stated plainly

The model sees 14 per-100, z-scored-within-season box-score stats per player-season. That is all:

  • No lineup data. The model does not know who shared the floor with whom.
  • No matchup context. It does not know who guarded whom, or the score when a stat happened.
  • Defense is represented only via steals and blocks. Everything a defender does that isn't a steal or a block is invisible to the model.

What the model cannot see, it cannot know. Two players with identical stat lines get identical vectors even if one anchored a defense and the other hunted highlights. The map is faithful to its input — and its input is the box score, not the game.

source: stat-scope statement per overhaul plan §6b (Oliver's scope-honesty amendment).

Plate 03 · how "nearby" is measured

Similarity is cosine similarity — the angle between two 64-number arrows. Pointing the same direction: near 1, very similar style. At right angles: near 0, unrelated style. It ignores how long the arrows are (overall volume) and cares only about their direction (the shape of the game).

Why the angle and not the distance? Because a high-usage scorer and a low-usage scorer can play the same style at different volumes. Cosine similarity hears the shape of the game through the noise of minutes.

Honesty note. These similarity scores are uncalibrated point estimates — there are no confidence intervals attached. A 0.886 and a 0.884 may not be meaningfully different. Treat close scores as ties, not rankings. More on /provenance.

Plate 04 · the scope sentence, everywhere it matters

Because it is easy to slide from "similar style" to "similar quality," the rule on this site is: wherever a model claim sits near value-language, the scope sentence (or its tighter form) sits with it.

"This map measures how players play, not how good they are." — or, tighter: "how they play, not how good they are." (The full form opens this page, above.)

Two examples of the rule in action: the atlas intro lede opens with it; the PLUS_MINUS stat is labeled an "on/off estimate (noisy)" rather than a rating; and a twin chip that once read "both tilt the floor" now reads "both outscore opponents on court" — a statement about what happened, not a crown.

source: scope-sentence placement spec, overhaul plan §6b; 2026-10-18 wording fixes per project records.
