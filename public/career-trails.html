career trails · reading the path

A career is a path through the map.

This map measures how players play, not how good they are — so a trail shows how a style evolved, not whether a player improved.

Every season of a player's career is one point in the 64-d space. Connect the seasons in order and you get a trail: the visible shape of how that player's game changed over time.

Plate 01 · how the trail maps to embedding movement

  • Each dot is a season. One player-season in, one 64-d vector out, one dot on the trail.
  • Consecutive seasons usually sit near each other. Most players evolve gradually, so the trail creeps — small steps year to year.
  • Long jumps mean the style changed. A new team, a new role, an injury, age — whatever the cause, a big move on the trail means the stat fingerprint moved. The trail shows that it moved, not why; the why lives in the box scores and the history books.
  • Loops and returns happen. A player who reinvents themselves late can wander back toward an earlier style — the trail can revisit old territory.

The animation is just the trail drawn in order: dot appears, line extends, next season. Nothing is interpolated or smoothed — every position on screen is a real season's real vector.

Plate 02 · the "what changed" strip

Under the trail, a strip names the two dimensions that moved most between the selected seasons — in plain words, not dimension indices. If a player's trail jumps between 2018-19 and 2019-20, the strip says what the model saw change: for example, a shift toward "more of the offense runs through him" or "fewer rebounds, more perimeter play."

How it works: for the two seasons' vectors, compute |a_d − b_d| on each of the 64 dimensions and take the two largest — the axes where the style moved furthest. Those two get the plain-words labels. It is a description of movement, not an explanation of cause: the strip says what changed in the stats, and you supply the story.

source: trail-to-movement mapping and "what changed" = 2 largest-moving dimensions, per overhaul plan §1.5.

Plate 03 · reading trails honestly

  • A long trail is not a great career. Distance on the map is style change, not value added. A journeyman who changed roles five times can out-travel a superstar who played one way for fifteen years.
  • A short trail is not stagnation. Staying in one corner of the map for a decade means a stable, repeatable style — often the mark of the very best.
  • The map has no up. There is no "better" direction in style-space. Movement is lateral by definition: different, not higher.

Honesty note. Because the input is 14 box-score stats, the trail cannot show what those stats miss — defensive positioning, leadership, gravity without the ball. A trail that looks flat may hide a real evolution the box score never recorded. What the model cannot see, it cannot draw.
